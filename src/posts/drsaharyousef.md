## Who Is Dr. Sahar Yousef?

Dr. Sahar Yousef is a cognitive neuroscientist and lecturer associated with UC Berkeley's Haas School of Business. According to her official Berkeley profile, she completed her undergraduate education and PhD at Berkeley. Her 2018 doctorate was in Vision Science, with research focused on cognitive and attention regulation training.

Her work is notable because it moves between academic research and practical education. Rather than treating productivity as a simple matter of willpower or time management, Yousef examines how attention, energy, technology, and the environment shape the quality of human work.

## What Does a Cognitive Neuroscientist Study?

Cognitive neuroscience studies the relationship between mental processes and the brain. Its subjects can include attention, memory, perception, learning, decision-making, reasoning, and cognitive control. Researchers may use behavioral experiments, neuroimaging, EEG, and related methods to study how cognition works.

Yousef's doctoral research focused on <strong>Cognitive and Attention Regulation Training</strong>. Berkeley's alumni records identify Michael Silver as her doctoral adviser. That background helps explain why her public work treats focus as a cognitive process that can be supported by training and environment, rather than only as a motivational problem.

## From Brain Science to Better Work

Yousef's work sits at the intersection of neuroscience and modern knowledge work. Her research and teaching address questions such as:

- How does attention become fragmented by constant context switching?
- What role does memory play in learning and performance?
- How do notifications and digital stimulation compete for attention?
- Why can a long workday still produce little meaningful progress?
- How should people use breaks and recovery to protect energy?
- How can technology be used intentionally instead of automatically?

This approach is relevant to students, developers, entrepreneurs, creators, executives, and anyone whose work depends on sustained attention.

## Why Multitasking Can Feel Productive

One of the most familiar themes in Yousef's work is the difference between being busy and making progress. People often describe checking messages while writing, switching between browser tabs, and answering email during a meeting as multitasking. In practice, many of these behaviors are rapid shifts between tasks.

Every shift creates a cost: the mind has to reconstruct what it was doing, remember the next step, and suppress competing information. For demanding work, protecting a period of uninterrupted attention can be more valuable than extending the number of hours spent at a desk.

The practical principle is straightforward:

## Why a Phone Can Affect Attention Even When It Is Not Being Used

The Raj Shamani conversation discusses digital hygiene and the way smartphones can compete for attention even when a person is not actively scrolling. A visible phone, a vibration, or the expectation of an incoming message can create a background demand on attention.

This does not mean technology is inherently harmful. Phones provide communication, navigation, education, work tools, and access to useful information. The more useful question is whether the device is helping a person choose where attention goes or repeatedly making that choice for them.

Simple environmental changes can help: silence non-essential alerts, move distracting apps away from the home screen, use scheduled check-in windows, and put the phone physically out of reach during demanding work.

## The Connection Between Attention and Memory

Memory is not separate from attention. If information is never fully attended to, it is less likely to be encoded clearly enough to retrieve later. This is why more hours of studying do not automatically produce better learning when those hours are repeatedly interrupted.

Yousef's research background connects cognitive training with attention, memory, information processing, and overall performance. The practical lesson is not that memory can be upgraded by a single trick. It is that learning improves when people protect attention, work with manageable information, and return to material deliberately.

## Breaks, Recovery, and the 3M Framework

The podcast episode introduces Yousef's 3M framework for breaks: **Micro, Meso, and Macro breaks**. The categories describe recovery at different time scales rather than treating every break as the same.

- **Micro breaks:** brief pauses during a work period to reset attention or change physical state.
- **Meso breaks:** longer recovery periods between demanding blocks of work, meetings, or study.
- **Macro breaks:** substantial time away from work that supports broader recovery and prevents work from becoming continuous.

The larger idea is that sustainable performance depends on energy management as well as time management. A person can spend more hours working while producing less useful output if fatigue has already degraded attention and judgment.

## The Becoming Superhuman Lab and Public Education

Yousef runs the Becoming Superhuman Lab, which translates research and teaching into education about productivity, digital dependence, burnout prevention, focus, and energy management. UC Berkeley says she has delivered hundreds of talks and keynotes to knowledge workers and executives in more than 50 countries.

Berkeley and Yousef's biography also note that she was selected by Thinkers50 as one of the top 30 management thinkers to watch in 2024. Her work has been featured by outlets including <em>Forbes</em>, <em>The Wall Street Journal</em>, Bloomberg, CNBC, and <em>The New York Times</em>, according to UC Berkeley Executive Education.
